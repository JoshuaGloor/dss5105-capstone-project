SLIDE 2 - Problem (CHEN ZEPENG, 0:35)
Today, that decision is one line in a group chat: send it to QuickStitch. Behind it, three

SLIDE 3 - Solution and architecture (CHEN ZEPENG, 0:50)
So we split the job. It's a single-agent workflow, not ReAct or multi-agent: every request
needs the same checks in the same order, so control flow lives in code. One: the LLM, via
LangChain, returns structured output, a typed patch against a JSON schema. Every field
must quote the message exactly, or the system will reject the turn: a grounding guardrail that
fails closed. Two: deterministic tools, eligibility from the course harness, queues,
delivery estimates, a weighted ranking. All the arithmetic is wrapped in tool calls, so
the model will only gather the results from tools and make the response. Three: human in the loop. Confirm recomputes the
preview in one SQLite transaction, then writes the audit record. Session state is the
short-term memory, SQLite is the long-term memory, and only the model call crosses this trust boundary.

SLIDE 4 - Primary objective (0:35)
We declare one objective: minimise lateness. In quick response, speed is the product: a
defect can be reworked, a missed reorder date loses the sale. We count late batches and
late days. Here is the scoreboard, wins and losses. Lateness: we beat all three baselines.
Defects: biggest-capacity still beats us. A surprise closure moves us a quarter of a
point; random moves four. Fairness: random spreads work more evenly, but it is late a
third of the time.

SLIDE 5 - Business value (0:30)
What does that buy? Out of 120 batches, 2 arrive late instead of 41: ninety-five percent
fewer than random. That's the course simulator, not a factory. And no batch goes where it
can't be made, every decision is on record, and an impossible date is flagged on day one,
with options. The price: about a third more than the cheapest rule, which is late nine
times in ten.

SLIDE 6 - Progress (0:35)
The shipped baselines run a third to nine-tenths late; we're under two percent. The
honest part: a ten-second earliest-finish rule ties us, because two batches of 2,000
pieces simply can't be made in their window. So what we add isn't a better formula, it's
configuration. The same code, re-weighted, buys 7.7 percent lower cost and 23 percent
fewer late days for one more late batch per ten runs. And it's deployed: every merge
builds an image to ECR, and one workflow deploys it to AWS.
