SLIDE 1 - Opening (CHEN ZEPENG, 0:25)
Good evening, we're Group 11. SweaterCo is a knitwear factory that lives on quick
reorders. When the factory is full, the next batch has to go to one of these eight
workshops within the hour. We built the desk that makes that call, shows its working and
keeps the record. Our primary objective is to minimise lateness.

SLIDE 2 - Problem (CHEN ZEPENG, 0:40)
Today, that decision is one line in a group chat: send it to QuickStitch. Behind it,
someone is answering three questions from memory. Can they make it? Half of them can't
make a scarf. Can they take it now? QuickStitch, the fastest, is booked solid for four
days. Are they allowed to? OldMill is suspended, and FreshStart is on trial, capped at 300
pieces. And only 7 of this morning's 30 messages are straightforward. Get one wrong and
the batch bounces back a day later, which in a ten-day cycle wipes out the margin.

SLIDE 3 - Solution and architecture (CHEN ZEPENG, 0:40)
So instead of one person juggling all that, we built one dispatcher agent, run as a fixed
workflow. On the left, the model reads the message through LangChain. It's the only thing
beyond this dashed line, and it never sees a price or a queue. Every value must quote the
message's exact words, or Python rejects it, and anything missing becomes a question, not
a guess. In the middle, Python tools work out who is eligible and when each workshop would
deliver. On the right, a person approves. The session remembers the conversation; the
database remembers every decision.

SLIDE 4 - Primary objective (0:35)
We declare one objective: minimise lateness. In quick response, speed is the product: a
defect can be reworked, a missed reorder date loses the sale. We count late batches and
late days. Here is the scoreboard, wins and losses. Lateness: we beat all three baselines.
Defects: biggest-capacity still beats us. A surprise closure moves us a quarter of a
point; random moves four. Fairness: random spreads work more evenly, but it is late a
third of the time.

SLIDE 5 - Business value (0:30)
What does that buy? Out of 120 batches, 2 arrive late instead of 41: ninety-five percent
fewer than random. That's the course simulator, not a factory. And no batch goes where it
can't be made, every decision is on record, and an impossible date is flagged on day one,
with options. The price: about a third more than the cheapest rule, which is late nine
times in ten.

SLIDE 6 - Progress (0:35)
The shipped baselines run a third to nine-tenths late; we're under two percent. The
honest part: a ten-second earliest-finish rule ties us, because two batches of 2,000
pieces simply can't be made in their window. So what we add isn't a better formula, it's
configuration. The same code, re-weighted, buys 7.7 percent lower cost and 23 percent
fewer late days for one more late batch per ten runs. And it's deployed: every merge
builds an image to ECR, and one workflow deploys it to AWS.

SLIDE 7 - The desk today (0:25)
This is the desk on request R25: five hundred vests, keep it away from BudgetWorks. The
exclusion is recorded against Mei, who asked. Under the pick, the desk says why, in lines
Python writes, not the model. All thirty inbox requests reach the right outcome from
hand-checked inputs; testing the live model on them is our first Sprint 2 task.

SLIDE 8 - Risks and assumptions (0:30)
The worst failure in this track is an invented delivery estimate; our design prevents it
and we check it automatically. The likeliest is a misread request: in our first live run,
a needless question blocked a refusal. And no message can talk past the rules: eligibility
is Python, not the model. Our key assumption: a person confirms every allocation.

SLIDE 9 - Sprint 2 roadmap (0:25)
Sprint 2 makes the desk AI-native, while tools keep every number. The evaluation set comes
first, with injection and hallucination bait. Then the model takes on three jobs: choosing
a read-only tool for questions, explaining decisions, and drafting replies, with Python
filling in every number. Our bar: the right behaviour on at least twenty-seven of thirty,
with no untraceable number, while still beating every baseline. Thank you.
